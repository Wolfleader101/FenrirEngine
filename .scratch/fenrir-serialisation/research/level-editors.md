# What makes a good level editor

Research for [What makes a good level editor?](../issues/05-what-makes-a-good-level-editor.md) (wayfinder `fenrir-serialisation`). Read 2026-11-10.

Every finding is phrased as **what a tool must let a designer do**, so that [What do level editors demand of a scene format and engine API?](../issues/06-what-level-editors-demand-of-the-data.md) can read each want against Fenrir's data layer. Each claim cites a numbered source from the Sources section; versions and dates are pinned there. Claims drawn from search excerpts of pages that could not be fetched directly (the Valve Developer Community and the Bethesda Creation Kit wikis sit behind bot checks today) are marked *excerpt* and should be re-verified before anything is built on them alone.

Two framing points before the bullets:

- **Designers judge editors by UX, not by the geometry technology.** Robin-Yann Storm (tool UX designer on Hitman's Glacier 2 and Guerrilla's Decima editors) argues the Hammer-versus-Unreal debate "is about user experience, not underlying technology", that editors should be judged by how easily users "place, edit, and iterate on content", and that fixing one UX issue "can save 20 tool users an hour a day" [7]. Epic's Jim Brown puts the same thing as "Instant gratification, feedback loop - Iteration is king" and warns "Own the toolset or it will own you" [11].
- **Geometry is not the whole level.** Robert Yang calls the over-emphasis on layout "the architectural fallacy"; the tools that most shape player behaviour are scripting, pacing, encounters, items and economy, and "You can't make levels for a game that doesn't exist" [12e]. An editor is therefore only as good as its support for placing and wiring gameplay, not only for cutting walls.

## Workflows

### Greyboxing and blockout

- **Drag out a solid in one gesture, on a grid, and see its size as you do it.** Storm's concrete comparison: Hammer shows "the geometry, its measurements, and its current location all in one glance in the middle of the screen", whereas in Unreal "we don't see the measurements, we have to go into a menu, change variables there" and then reposition the pivot; his conclusion is that "Hammer currently has the fastest way to build, iterate, and texture basic geometry" [7]. Unreal users say the same over a decade: brushes "generally produce very square results" and "aligning and pushing those brushes together is quite tedious" (2014) [13a]; "editing CSG in Unreal (including 4) sucks" and "its geometry tools are poorly implemented and don't allow rapid and carefree iteration cycles", to which Epic's Tim Hobson replied that a redo was planned with "no specific timeline" [13b]; in 2025 a designer still contrasts Hammer and J.A.C.K., where "simple brushes can be dragged out and shaped quickly", with Unreal, where moving ten or more BSP pieces together becomes "so laggy as to be unusable" [13c].
- **Build with convex brushes and CSG, and get validity for free.** TrenchBroom offers convex merge ("computes the convex hull of all vertices of those brushes"), subtraction ("creates brushes that represent the concave shape by cutting up the minuend brushes"), intersection ("the largest brush that is contained in each of the input brushes") and hollow ("walls will have a thickness of the current grid size"), plus vertex, edge and face tools [1]. The Level Design Book recommends it "for its construction capabilities alone": "Clipping planes can be 3D, and all brushes are automatically validated for convexity", and it "is also great at resizing many brushes at once" [12c]. Unreal's own docs now say brushes are "perfect" for blocking out a shell before art exists, but that their role "has been passed on to Static Meshes and in-engine modeling tools, which are far more efficient", and that brush vertices must sit on the drag grid or rendering artefacts follow [2e]; its replacement, CubeGrid, "creates blockout meshes using a repositionable grid" with push/pull hotkeys "helpful in quickly building level prototypes", and is still labelled Beta in 5.9 [2h].
- **Make blockout geometry solid immediately.** The book's playtest rule is "full gravity, collision, and speed, not flying in the editor" [12a]. Unreal designers in 2026 still have to fix this after the fact: a new user's blockout had no collision ("the player could walk through them") and the answer was to batch-set "Use Complex Collision As Simple" in the Property Matrix [13d]. Brush-based editors do not have this step: Epic's own 2010 slides list "Quick Collision" as why BSP was "Quick and Easy" for prototyping [11].
- **Snap to a coarse, player-sized grid and change the grid size without leaving the viewport.** TrenchBroom's grid runs 0.125 to 256 in powers of two, "most editing operations will be snapped to the grid", and the size changes from the mouse wheel with modifiers [1]. The book's rule: "Set a coarse grid based on player width" (Quake 32u player, grid 32/64; Unity 1m, grid 1/2; Unreal 60uu, grid 50/100 or 64/128), walls at 150-200% of player height, entryways at least two figure widths, and "Modular kits should use a grid equal to the module size" [12a]. Unreal users asked for configurable grid sizes in 2014 [13a].
- **Throw the blockout away cheaply.** "It is 'cheap' to delete or rebuild some rough blockout geometry, but throwing away finalized art passed work is 'expensive' and wasteful" and "99% of the time, your blockout will not survive a playtest" [12a]. Burgess's pass structure makes the same point institutional: Pass One is "complete layout, basic rhythm and flow" with "No optimization, navmesh, encounters, or lighting", and the deck says "Expose Problems - Don't Cover Up" and "Minimize pain of later changes" [9].
- **Texture the blockout fast, with alignment that survives edits.** TrenchBroom keeps UVs stable across vertex edits with UV Lock ("TrenchBroom will attempt to keep vertex UV coordinates the same"), while alignment lock for shearing "only works in Valve 220 format maps" because that format stores per-face UV axes rather than paraxial projection [1]. Source 2 adds hotspot materials, which "try to align themselves to mesh edges" and are applied by edge or by face (*excerpt*) [21].

### Kits, props and kitbashing

- **Snap kit pieces to a footprint grid and to each other.** Bethesda builds on footprints that are multiples of one another ("a 512x512x512 room will always tile nicely with a 256x256x256 hallway", while 384 "will eventually create gaps and/or overlaps"), designers "build on a grid snap setting of one-half the size of the footprint", and "it is very important to have your grid snap sizes as large as possible" [8]. Beyond the world grid, the Creation Kit's Snap to Reference means "the editor can treat any object as the origin for things to snap for", so a rotated piece becomes a new grid [8]. The Fallout 4 Creation Kit's grid snap defaults to 64 units and reference snap is a hotkey toggle (*excerpt*) [16c].
- **Keep pivots and footprints stable, because thousands of placed instances depend on them.** Pivots are "at the ground plane of the kit and in the center" and "Changing pivots later can be a huge problem, requiring manual updates to hundreds of instances" [8]; "It's generally bad news to change certain aspects of a kit once levels are built with it" [10]. Kit pieces are "heavily instanced, so changes appear across the game immediately", which lets artists "see art in real use without building a test area" [8].
- **Mix kits freely, and keep variants and patch pieces honest.** Kitbashing ("kit-jamming, or a kit mashup") is how repetition is beaten, but "patch pieces" "are often workarounds that subvert the core logic of the kit" [8][10]; the Level Design Book's kit checklist says "DO NOT MAKE VARIANTS YET, just build basic pieces first" and that designers and kit artists "need to agree on a pattern" for naming [12b].
- **Browse and place from a palette without leaving the viewport.** Halo Infinite's Forge keeps an Object Browser whose categories "can be opened and closed, and their state will persist until the Forge session is closed", a Recents list that persists between sessions, and a Prefabs section from which prefabs "can be placed on the map straight from the menu" [14d]. Epic's 2010 slides credit the Generic Browser as the "Awesome tool that brings everything you need to one location" [11].
- **Scale objects rather than ship every size.** Halo Infinite's Forge added object scaling, "eliminating the need to summon the same objects of different sizes", and static/dynamic flags per object [14c].

### Wiring gameplay

- **Connect any entity's output to any entity's input, with a parameter, a delay and a fire count, from the property sheet of either end.** Source's `CEventAction` holds exactly this: "name of the entity(s) to cause the action in", "the name of the action to fire", "parameter to send, 0 if none", "the number of seconds to wait before firing the action" and "The number of times to fire this event, or EVENT_FIRE_ALWAYS" [5b]. Hammer shows connections on both ends: the Inputs tab lists what an entity receives, double-clicking an input "moves you to the Outputs tab of the entity that sent it", and the available inputs, outputs and keyvalues come from the loaded FGD (*excerpt*) [5c].
- **See a broken connection the moment it breaks, and fire any input by hand while playing.** Invalid I/O connections "are highlighted red in the entity's input or output list" and also show in Check For Problems; `ent_fire` lets the designer fire inputs on any entity at any time and `developer` steps I/O one event at a time (*excerpt*) [5c]. Epic's 2010 slides give the same reason for Kismet: visual scripting is "easier to debug than lines of code or huge text files", and it let designers work without "huge teams, script compiles, cross department support" [11].
- **Script in a node graph that is budgeted, logged and shareable.** Forge's Node Graph caps each script brain at "128 nodes and 512 connections", its Global Log "records node-related build messages and reports when a graph fails to build", object references are added "when objects are selected", and from CU29 mode logic "will no longer come with a map budget cost" and ships as Mode Prefabs made only of Mode Brains [14a][14b][14e]. Dreams wires gadgets to each other and to sculptures and groups them in microchips (*excerpt*) [17].
- **Reuse wired logic without the names colliding.** Source's instancing renames entities per instance ("InstanceAuto%d" unless the instance is named) and remaps every I/O target through the same fixup, and `replace` parameters are substituted "before entity fixup, so fixup may occur on the replaced value" [5a]. TrenchBroom's linked groups do not do this: the manual says "It is important not to think of linked groups as instancing", and the community's MESS tool adds `{_tb_group}` so "different names in different instances" are possible [1][19].
- **Keep logic entities out of the way of geometry.** Godot's advice for scene-to-scene coupling is signals up and calls down ("Extremely safe, but should be used only to 'respond' to behavior, not start it"), siblings "should only be aware of their own hierarchies", and a scene can implement `_get_configuration_warnings()` so the editor shows a warning icon instead of relying on external documentation, which "is error-prone by default" [4c].

### Composing open worlds

- **Load and edit a region, never the whole world.** Unreal's World Partition keeps "a single persistent level" divided into streamable grid cells; "To support the development of large worlds, the world is initially unloaded", only actors with Is Spatially Loaded disabled (backdrops, managers) appear on open, and the designer loads regions from the World Partition window or with Location Volumes [2b]. The Creation Kit's cells are "virtual spaces" loaded through a Cell View window per worldspace; exterior cells are "arranged in a three-dimensional grid", and interior cells link to exteriors through "Teleport" links between door objects [16a].
- **Define cells, streaming sources and HLODs in the editor, and have references across cells be safe.** Cell Size and Loading Range live in World Settings; Player Controllers are streaming sources by default and any actor can carry a Streaming Source component; "Actors that reference each other are loaded together"; actors referenced from the Level Blueprint "are marked as Always Loaded", so Epic steers designers to Blueprint classes instead [2b]. HLODs and the minimap are built by commandlets, not by hand [2b].
- **Paint roads and rivers as splines that reshape the terrain and carry meshes.** Landscape splines serve "roads, paths, fences, and other long, contiguous features that conform naturally to the landscape"; the terrain "is raised or lowered to fit the spline, with a smooth cosine-blended falloff", painted layers are set per segment, meshes are assigned per segment or control point, and the workflow is "non-destructive" [2f]. Worldspaces in the Creation Kit have "an editable terrain mesh" and water at a set elevation [16a].
- **Paint foliage and scatter with density, randomisation and surface filters, and get instancing for free.** Unreal's Foliage Mode paints Static Mesh foliage that "can render in a single draw call", with Paint, Erase (Shift), Reapply, Lasso, Point Density, scale ranges, Align to Normal and placement filters (Landscape, Static Mesh, BSP, Foliage, Translucent); the default Instanced Foliage Grid under World Partition is 256 m, and "the entire cluster of instances change LODs simultaneously" [2g].
- **Let procedural tools fill what no designer will touch, inside the same editor.** Far Cry 5's tools existed because "Not every square meter of the world will receive the attention of a level artist or a level designer"; they generated biomes, textured terrain, "set up freshwater networks, generate cliff rocks and more", and ran through Houdini Engine "fully integrated into the Ubisoft game editor" so artists stayed in the editor they knew [18]. The Level Design Book warns the same tools are a distraction for level design proper: avoid "Erosion or auto-generator tools" because they remove design control, and beware "Sculpting distraction" and "Spline distraction" [12a]. Burgess: procedural tools "require careful rule-crafting and input" [10].
- **Compose a world from nested scenes.** Godot's open-world example goes "from rooms to houses, citadels, and terrain" by nesting scene instances; a saved scene "works as a blueprint: you can reproduce it in other scenes as many times as you'd like" [4b]. Unreal's Level Instances exist for "points of interest and standalone gameplay setups", Packed Level Actors for "static buildings and dense visual setups" [2c].

### Authoring puzzles under constraints

- **Offer a constrained palette whose items know where they may go.** Portal 2's Puzzle Creator is "block based": surfaces are pushed and pulled with + and -, the palette holds buttons, droppers, turrets, lasers, gels and lights, "The entry door, exit door, and large observation room are mandatory items", "Some item placements are disallowed, such as items placed in midair or inside of other items", and items that need clearance behind their mounting surface show a dashed outline for it [15a].
- **Wire items by a single key and let one receiver take many inputs.** Right-click connects items, pressing K is the quick connect, and "Multiple buttons can feed one receiver, which then needs all of them pressed at once" [15a].
- **Build and play with one key, and show a budget bar.** The top bar's "Build and play puzzle" button and F9 rebuild and run; `puzzlemaker_compile_and_preview` loads the map in preview; a budget bar shows how close the chamber is to the engine limit of 1750 entities (against 2048 in Hammer maps) [15a].
- **Let the designer grow out of the constrained tool.** Export is one-way: `puzzlemaker_export` writes a VMF for Hammer, which "can be overcome by exporting the map to Hammer", but "Hammer files can't be brought back into Puzzle Maker" and the exported file is "almost impossible-to-edit logic and unoptimized brushwork" (*excerpt*) [15a][5d]. Under the hood items are Hammer instances compiled through VBSP and VRAD; the community's first complaints were that the palette and the editor's dimensions were fixed ("I have an extremely hard time that it isn't possible to change the dimensions/tabs on the editor") and the community answered with BEE2, which reconfigures the editor "so it can use additional items and reskin maps" [15b][15c].
- **Treat UGC tools as the same problem with a budget UI.** Dreams splits its budget into Gameplay, Graphics and Audio thermometers and expects creators to "keep your levels as low-cost as possible" [17c]; Forge shows a Budget Meter that warns at 80% and turns red at 100%, and in Play Mode shows "the runtime budget that is the fullest at any given moment" [14a][14f]. When a Forge limit is hit "the engine stops creating more instances of that type of entity" [14a].

### The edit-play-edit loop

- **Play from the editor in seconds, and keep playing while editing.** Epic's slides list "Real-time editing and PIE" and moving objects "without need to rebuild and cook" [11]; Unreal 5.9 lets the designer pause, eject, edit an actor's property in Details and see it "immediately in the viewport" [2i]; Dreams' test mode lets creators "grab and possess" as in play mode while using tweak menus "as in edit mode", and tweak changes "carry over when you rewind" but sculpture changes do not [17b]; Forge's Play Mode is one key (F6) and bots "are able to interact with the Forge canvas while in test mode through nav-mesh" [14c][14f].
- **Make it explicit which play-time changes survive.** Unity: "In Play mode, any changes you make are temporary and are reset when you exit Play mode" [3g]. Unreal: "Changes you make to Actors in the Level using this method are not saved by default", and Keep Simulation Changes (K) copies properties "from the runtime copy back to the editor copy" because the editor "creates a duplicate copy of your level" for the session; community reports say it "will often only copy back the most recent property changed" and misses components created during play (*excerpt*) [2i][22].
- **Compile or build only the part being worked on.** Hammer's cordon hides everything outside a dragged box and compiles only what is inside, sealing the hole; the designer must put a player start inside (*excerpt*) [5e][6b]. Hidden visgroups are also excluded from compilation, "so you can playtest one section quickly", though "you still need a skybox around your section, otherwise the map won't compile" [6a]. World Partition's load-a-region behaviour serves the same need at open-world scale [2b].
- **Launch the engine from the editor with a reproducible compile profile.** TrenchBroom's compile profiles have "a name, a working directory, and a list of tasks" (Export Map, Run Tool, Launch Engine, Copy Files, Rename, Delete), "A test run will only print what each task will do without actually executing it", and engines must be registered before launch [1]. Source 2's tools "are now integrated into the game", so "the game must be run in order to use Hammer" (*excerpt*) [5f].
- **Find leaks and visibility problems in the editor, not in a log.** TrenchBroom loads QBSP point files "which help locate leaks" as green line segments the camera can walk along, and portal files that "let you visualize the portals between BSP leafs" [1].
- **Get an autosave and unlimited undo.** TrenchBroom: "Almost everything that you do in TrenchBroom can be undone", "There is no limit to how many actions you can undo", related actions are grouped into transactions, backups go to an `autosave` folder with at most 50 kept, and a crash "saves a crash report and the map file automatically" [1]. Unreal designers in 2014 reported undo with many brushes taking minutes [13a]. Forge's folder-structure changes "can't be undone with the undo action" [14g].

## Organisation

- **Hide, lock and isolate any subset, by hand-made group and by automatic category, and nest the groups.** Hammer's visgroups put objects "onto a layer"; Hammer also auto-assigns every prop, brush and entity to Auto visgroups; an object can belong to several groups; groups merge by drag and nest by right-drag; "Mark" selects a group's contents; and Valve recommends "no more than 128 VisGroups" [6a]. TrenchBroom's layers "partition a map into several large areas in order to reduce visual clutter by hiding them", can be hidden, locked or omitted from export, new objects go to the current layer, and objects created from others "inherit the source object's layer" [1]. Unity's Scene Visibility hides "without changing their visibility at runtime" and isolates "selected GameObjects in the Scene view to temporarily hide all other GameObjects" [3e]. Fallout 4's Creation Kit added layers shown or hidden by double-click (*excerpt*) [16c]. Forge's Folders lock and hide per folder; locked objects "can't be selected in the viewport but can still be grabbed from the Folders tab", selecting a folder "doesn't select its contents directly", and "A maximum of 150 objects can be in a viewport selection" [14g][14f].
- **Separate editor-only organisation from runtime layers, and let one actor be in several.** Unreal's Data Layers come in Editor layers ("keep your assets organized within your project") and Runtime layers that Blueprints or C++ toggle for "quests, progression, in-game events, and level transitions"; Data Layer Assets are project-level and "not world specific", instances are per world, layers nest, and the example use is "Separating gameplay elements from environment art so artists can work without touching gameplay triggers" [2d]. Caution from the same page: "Widely used assets assigned to too many Runtime Data Layer Instances can degrade World Partition streaming performance" [2d].
- **Group objects into one editable unit with a name.** TrenchBroom: "groups are used to merge a small number of objects into one object so that they can all be edited as one", and "Groups allow you to treat several objects as one and to give them a name" [1]. Forge welds objects, with "Welds cannot contain static objects" [14f].
- **Instance a sub-map in place, edit it in context, and choose per instance what is overridden.** Source's `func_instance` loads a separate VMF, transforms brushes, displacements, overlays and entities, remaps names and I/O, substitutes `replace` variables, and collapses everything into the main map at compile [5a]. Unreal's Level Instances are edited in context with the rest of the level greyed out, committed with Commit Changes, and "Any changes made to a Level Instance propagate to all copies"; breaking one "cannot be undone"; in Embedded mode OFPA instances are discarded at runtime and their actors join the World Partition grid [2c]. Unity's prefab mode edits in isolation or in context ("The rest of the current working scene remains visible, but locked for editing"), overrides are visualised with Show Overrides, variants inherit from a base where "Overrides in the variant take precedence over the base values" and "A variant can use any prefab as its base, including other variants" [3b][3c]. Godot: "Changing a property on an instance always overrides values from the corresponding packed scene", with a revert icon per property, and shared resources must be made unique before an instance can edit them [4b]. TrenchBroom's linked groups copy contents between duplicates, with "protected" properties that block propagation to a given copy [1]. Source 2 prefabs are edited in place by double-clicking inside the map (*excerpt*) [21]. Forge warns that "Adding new objects to an Object Prefab will reset the prefab name and make it an entirely new asset" [14f].
- **Reference across maps by a stable identity, not a path or a name.** Godot's `uid` "is a unique string-based identifier representing the scene" so the engine tracks a file when it moves; Godot 4 replaced "incremental integer IDs" with string IDs, and nodes gained a `unique_id` in 4.6 [4a]. Unreal keeps every OFPA actor in an external file with an encoded name, so source control shows encoded names and the Editor's View Changelist window shows actor names instead [2a].
- **Share a map without exclusive locks on the whole file.** Unreal's One File Per Actor exists because editing an actor used to mean checking out the whole level, "which locked out teammates"; OFPA "reduces overlap between users by saving data for instances of Actors in external files", and all actors "are embedded in their respective Level files when cooked" [2a]. Unity's alternative is a semantic merge of `.unity`, `.prefab` and `.meta` files governed by `mergerules.txt`, needed "when two people change the same scene or prefab file in parallel"; exclusive checkout removes the need [3d]. Godot's TSCN files "have the advantage of being mostly human-readable and easy for version control systems to manage" [4a]. Unity's multi-scene editing is pitched both for "large streaming worlds" and because it "lets you improve the workflow, especially if you often have to edit scenes collaboratively" [3f]. Forge adds a version note on every save "which helps with documenting changes and collaboration" [14a]; Dreams relies on invited collaborators and remix notifications rather than merging [17d].
- **Keep plugins and overrides layered, and make unintended overrides visible.** Bethesda mods are plugins layered on masters ("At a minimum the 'Starfield.esm' master file must be loaded before saving a mod plugin"), so a record is either inherited or overridden [16b]. The cost: the Creation Kit "can set the modified flag on a thing very easily", producing identical-to-master records that silently cancel another mod's change, and deleted references that "are a significant cause of crashes and game bugs", which the community cleans with xEdit; "If your mod contains intentional ITMs, then there is something wrong with it" [16d].

## Precision

- **Grid in powers of two, snapping on by default, per-operation snap control.** TrenchBroom (above) [1]; the Creation Kit's grid and angle snap with a 64-unit default (*excerpt*) [16c]; Forge's movement and scale snaps run from none to 16 feet and rotation from half a degree to 180, in World, Object or Camera space [14f]. Unreal's docs make off-grid brush vertices a rendering bug [2e].
- **Snap to another object, not only to the world grid.** Creation Kit Snap to Reference [8]; Hammer-style editors snap component-wise during a move [1].
- **Give the designer two ways to read dimensions: 2D orthographic views with measurements, and the 3D view.** Storm's praise for Hammer is specifically the measurements shown in the viewport while dragging [7]; Forge's community wiki lists "There is no orthographic 2D view option in Halo Infinite's Forge" among its limits [14f]; TrenchBroom deliberately moved to "3D construction in a single view pane" and the book still recommends it [12c].
- **Edit vertices, edges and faces directly, with convexity preserved, and clip with a 3D plane.** TrenchBroom's three tools, with "fusing is not allowed when you are moving edges or faces" as the only guard [1]; the Radiant-vs-Hammer complaint that "in the UDK/UE4/Hammer need select the sides to move" while Radiant is "automatic" shows how much a click of friction matters [13b].
- **Resize and move many objects at once without lag.** Praised in TrenchBroom [12c]; the recurring Unreal complaint is exactly this failing [13a][13c].
- **Keep textures aligned across edits.** UV Lock and Valve 220 per-face UV axes [1]; Source 2 hotspots (*excerpt*) [21]; Bethesda's rotated kit pieces produce "tiny holes that you might only see at just the right angle" and texture seams that need attention [8].
- **Selection that respects locks and hides, and scales to hundreds of objects.** Forge's 150-object selection cap (255 with prefabs) is reported as a limit to route around [14g]; Unity's Scene Picking is a separate per-object flag alongside visibility [3e].
- **Give gizmos single-axis grabs that are easy to hit.** CU29's quality-of-life item was making it "easier to grab a selection axis" on the scale and transform widgets [14b].

## Feedback

- **A live problem list with quick fixes.** TrenchBroom's issue browser is "a live list of problems TrenchBroom detects in the map, and it updates as the map changes", issues can be fixed, hidden or shown, and the manual is honest that "TrenchBroom cannot detect all issues that may lead to compilation errors or warnings" [1]. Hammer's Check For Problems and red I/O rows do the same for wiring (*excerpt*) [5c]. Godot's configuration warnings put the same idea on a node in the Scene dock [4c].
- **Budgets on screen while editing and while playing.** Forge's Budget Meter and Runtime Budget Meter, with named categories (Dynamic, Total, Vehicles, FX Count, Reflection Volumes, Movers, Animations, Physics, Collision, Static) and a separate Forge Simulation budget for "prefabs, user strings, and Node Graph elements" [14a][14f]; Dreams' three thermometers [17c]; the Puzzle Creator's budget bar [15a]. Each folder in Forge "adds to the Forge Simulation budget", so organisation itself is budgeted [14g].
- **Lighting that updates live, and a clear statement of what is baked.** Epic's 2010 slides: Lightmass colour and brightness "can be updated on the fly in real time" [11]. Halo Infinite's Forge removed baked lighting "in favour of static and dynamic shadow settings" and added "Light and shadow heatmaps" for performance [14c]. Forge's community wiki has separate "Building Lighting", "Building Nav Mesh", "Building Audio" and "Building Reflection Volumes" steps, so builds are explicit [14f].
- **Navmesh the designer can see, generate, and finish by hand.** Bethesda's workflow is generate, then "clean it up by hand, run Find Cover, and Finalize it", because cover edges are "not saved with finalized edge data" and "Ledge cover must be done by hand" (*excerpt*) [16e]. Burgess's Pass Two is where "navmesh for AI pathing" first appears, not Pass One [9]. Forge bots path over the navmesh in test mode [14c].
- **Debug visualisation of wiring and streaming.** Source's `ent_messages` style debugging and `developer` stepping (*excerpt*) [5c]; Forge's Global Log for "Node Graph errors and warnings" in Play Mode [14f]; World Partition's Preview Grids and data-layer debug colours [2b][2d].
- **Watch the player, not the editor.** The book's playtest guidance: "Watching their behavior is more important than their feedback", stat collection "does not require any special plugins or tools, just attentive note-taking", and "normies have a hard time playtesting blockouts" [12d]. Burgess's team compiles group critiques, peer review and lead notes and "looks for trends" [9].

## Anti-patterns

- **Slow or laggy iteration on basic geometry.** The single most repeated complaint across twelve years of Unreal threads [13a][13b][13c], and Storm's central argument [7]. Epic itself calls BSP "not recommended as a final level design method" and points to Modeling Mode [2e].
- **Whole-file locks and binary merges.** The motivation Epic gives for OFPA ("locked out teammates") [2a] and Unity gives for Smart Merge [3d]. OFPA's own pitfalls: a user "can check out several files and submit only some, possibly leaving dangling references", changelist viewing "is only supported with Perforce", and sublevels do not convert automatically [2a].
- **Brittle references.** Breaking a Level Instance "cannot be undone"; Level Instances referencing a Data Layer Instance not in the world "will not have their Data Layer references carried over" [2c][2d]. Deleted references in Bethesda plugins crash games, so xEdit moves them to Z -30000 instead of deleting [16d]. VBSP's own instance code warns "Could not open instance file" and resolves instance paths by three different search rules, which is how an instance silently points at the wrong file [5a].
- **Hidden state and side effects.** The Creation Kit marks records modified "very easily", creating ITMs nobody intended [16d]. Godot discards `;` comments on save [4a]. Unity resets Play-mode edits [3g]; Unreal keeps them only on request and incompletely [2i][22]. Forge's "Hidden" "only applies to Test Mode", hidden objects cannot be selected on the map, deleting a folder deletes its objects, and prefabs silently become new assets when extended [14g][14f]. Puzzle Creator export is one-way [15a][5d].
- **Instancing that is not instancing.** TrenchBroom's linked groups duplicate rather than reference, so names collide unless a third-party tool rewrites them [1][19]; Unity's auto-save "can be slow" for complex prefabs and Transform cannot be edited in context [3c].
- **Tools that fight the engine's data model.** Portal 2's editor exports "unoptimized brushwork" and unreadable logic because its items are instances stitched by a compiler the editor does not understand [5d][15b]; Far Cry's answer was to put the procedural tools "within the game editor environment they're comfortable with" rather than beside it [18]; the GRAVEN team built in TrenchBroom and imported to Unreal via HammUEr, warning that exporting "a loose pile of brushes is a major time sink" and placing each piece at the origin so pivots line up (*excerpt*) [20].
- **Kits and metrics changed after content exists.** Pivot, footprint or transition changes "can break many layouts" [10]; "Changing pivots later can be a huge problem" [8].
- **Budgets that fill from the wrong thing.** Forge folders and prefabs cost simulation memory [14g]; a Reddit report of a map breaking at 18.7% of node graph budget (*excerpt*) [14h]; Puzzle Creator chambers hit 1750 entities [15a].
- **Architecture-only tooling.** Yang's warning that "speed level design" videos are "fine as art tests or fun asset jams, but don't call it level design!" [12e], and the book's warning that terrain and spline tools distract beginners from level design [12a].

## Data-facing wants at a glance

Derived from the bullets above, for ticket 06 to read against Fenrir's data layer. Each is a want, not a decision.

1. Brushes or an equivalent convex-solid primitive with CSG, that stay editable until a bake, with per-face UV axes [1][12c].
2. A player-metric grid and snap settings stored with the project, and snap-to-reference [1][8][12a].
3. Entity classes described outside the editor (FGD-like), with named inputs, outputs, parameters, delays and fire counts, and a validation pass that can flag a dangling target [5b][5c][1].
4. Instances of sub-scenes with per-instance overrides, name fixup and parameter substitution, collapsed or kept at runtime by policy [5a][2c][4b].
5. Layers, groups and visgroups that are editor-only, plus runtime layers that gameplay can toggle, with membership in several [6a][2d][1].
6. Stable, move-safe identities for files and for nodes [4a][2a].
7. A file layout that merges: text, one unit per actor or a semantic merge, no whole-file lock [2a][3d][4a].
8. Partial builds: cordon, hidden-excluded, region load [5e][6a][2b].
9. A play session that duplicates the scene and can copy chosen changes back [2i][3g].
10. Budgets and problems the editor can read live [14a][1][17c].
11. World data beyond entities: terrain, splines that deform it, foliage instances, navmesh with hand-finished edges, streaming cells [2f][2g][16e][2b].
